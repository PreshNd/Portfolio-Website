## The situation

Partners wanted to sell flights to their own customers without building a travel business. We called the offer OTaaS, Online Travel as a Service. A partner could plug into our infrastructure through a landing page, a micro-site, an iframe or a full white label.

Every partnership needed someone who understood both the deal and the system behind it. That person became me.

## My role

I picked up OTaaS in 2023, while I was still in the CMS role. It never left my table. I kept it through my time as Technical Product Coordinator and into product management. Whenever a partnership came in, it came to me.

## The lens I brought

I was the technical person for the Nigerian team. When a partner said what they wanted, I could see what it meant for the product, for engineering and for the operations team who would live with it after launch.

## Decisions I made

- **I matched each partner to the right product.** After the partner meetings, I decided what fitted the deal: a landing page, a micro-site, an iframe or a white label. Then I wrote the brief and the product specification.
- **I set up the plumbing myself.** In our back-office system I created each partner's affiliate and tracking IDs, set up UTM tracking, and applied the discounts the partnership called for.
- **I owned the whole build.** I collected brand guides, coordinated the front-end and back-end teams, tested everything and ran UAT. After launch, I connected our team with the partner's team for support.
- **I worked with many partners.** A few that stand out: MTN, whose landing page on the MTN marketplace app became a long-running way for customers to find us, and Zenith Bank. Both were among our top revenue partners. For a global Mastercard partnership, I managed the Nigerian implementation of a full micro-site. Uber and Samsung were partners too.
- **I ran a two-way partnership end to end.** With GIGM, we gave them flights and they gave us buses. I set up their flights white label from start to finish. Then I became the product owner for buses, a brand-new product for us. I wrote the proposition and the process documents, trained the operations team, answered their questions, and built a Looker Studio dashboard that I used to report on buses in management meetings.

## Payments

Payments ran through all of it. Some partners, like Zenith Bank, wanted only their own payment method on their white label, so payment-method rules were part of every specification I wrote. Engineering switched them on or off; I defined what each partner needed.

Paystack was our main integrated payment provider. I managed its technical side across B2C, B2B and B2B2C. When there was downtime or a customer's payment failed, I went through the logs, worked out whether it was an integration problem, and escalated to engineering with what I had found.

Quickteller's payment service moved to a new version of its API. I read the integration documentation, joined the conversations with their team, and wrote the requirements for our re-integration. It was ready and waiting for engineering capacity.

## What happened

Partners went live through every route we offered, from simple landing pages to a full micro-site and white labels. Buses gave us a new revenue stream alongside flights.

## What I learned

**The technical person is a product role.** Nobody wrote it into my job description. Anything technical came to me first, and that meant understanding the system, the partner and the team well enough to move each one forward.

**I learned payments by doing it.** Reading integration documents, tracing failed payments and writing requirements taught me more than any course. Next year, I'm making it formal with a payments product management course.
